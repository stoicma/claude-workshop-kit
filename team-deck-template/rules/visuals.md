# Visual rules

McKinsey-style decks have a small, disciplined visual vocabulary. Eight or so visual types cover 95% of slides. Every visual encodes one message. No decoration.

## Visual selection table

Match the visual to the argument:

| The slide argues... | Use visual type |
|---|---|
| One thing is bigger than others (size, share) | bar |
| Something changes over time (trend, growth) | line |
| Two trends differ across periods | column-grouped |
| Composition of a whole (segments, allocation) | pie or column-stacked |
| Two dimensions sort options (attractiveness vs feasibility) | matrix-2x3 |
| Three reasons / pillars / phases | 3-card |
| A vs B (build vs buy, before vs after) | comparison |
| Sequenced phases over time | roadmap |
| Risks and what to do about them | risk-table |
| Decisions needed with owners and deadlines | decision-table |

If the argument does not fit one of these, the slide is probably trying to say two things. Split it.

## Bar charts

Used for size comparison. Vertical bars when the categories are short (countries, segments, options). Horizontal bars when category labels are long.

McKinsey conventions:
- Bars in a single accent color, except the bar that matters (highlighted).
- Data labels at the top of each bar. Axis labels minimal.
- Source footer mandatory.
- Title is the conclusion, not the topic. "Market B leads in adoption at 47%" not "Adoption by market".

## Line charts

Used for trends over time. One series unless the comparison is the point (then 2 max, rarely 3).

McKinsey conventions:
- Smooth lines, no markers unless the data points themselves are the message.
- Highlight the inflection or threshold the slide argues for.
- X-axis is time, always.
- Title is the trend's takeaway, not "X over time".

## 2x3 matrices

Used to sort options or markets across two dimensions. Common dimensions: attractiveness × feasibility, opportunity × risk, size × growth.

McKinsey conventions:
- Cells are not equal-weight. The cell that contains the recommendation is highlighted.
- Each cell has a short label, never a paragraph.
- Both axis dimensions are spelled out. Never "good / bad", always specific dimensions.

## 3-card layouts

Used when the slide argues three reasons, pillars, phases, options. The most common McKinsey body layout for non-chart slides.

McKinsey conventions:
- Each card has a number (1, 2, 3) or icon at the top, then a short title (3-6 words), then one supporting line or stat.
- Cards are equal-width. Equal-height when possible.
- The title of each card is itself an action title. "JV closes regulatory gap in 18 months" not "Regulatory advantages".

## Comparison layouts

Used for binary decisions: build vs buy, organic vs JV, current state vs target state.

McKinsey conventions:
- Two equal columns. Top of each column is the option name.
- Below: 3-5 short bullets per side.
- The recommended option is highlighted (color or weight).

## Roadmap timelines

Used for sequenced phases of a program. Always horizontal, time runs left to right.

McKinsey conventions:
- 3-4 phases typically. Each phase is a labeled box with duration and outcome.
- Milestones marked between phases.
- The phase that matters most is highlighted.

## Risk and decision tables

Used at the end of the deck. Risk-table shows risk + mitigation. Decision-table shows decision + owner + deadline.

McKinsey conventions:
- 3 rows usually. Risks are concrete and quantified where possible.
- Mitigations are actions, not aspirations. "Phased capex tied to milestones" not "Monitor closely".

## What is NOT in the McKinsey visual vocabulary

- Stock photos
- Decorative icons (handshakes, lightbulbs, cogs)
- Word clouds
- Smartart shapes (gears, circular flow, etc.)
- Animated transitions
- 3D charts
- Multi-color palettes (more than 3 colors total)
- Gradients

## Color discipline

McKinsey's visual identity uses:
- Deep navy (#0A2540 or similar) for primary text and bars
- A single accent color (orange #E76F00 or muted blue #1A6FB8) used sparingly to highlight the point that matters
- Greys for secondary content
- White space as content

That's it. Three colors plus white. Any fourth color needs a reason.
